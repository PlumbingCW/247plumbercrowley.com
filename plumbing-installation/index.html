Codes, Permits and Inspections in Crowley

Crowley uses the 2021 International Plumbing Code. It also adopted the 2021 International Residential Code and the 2021 International Fuel Gas Code, with local amendments posted by the city. Our installs are built to those editions, so pipe sizing, venting, fixture clearances and gas connections match what the inspector will check.

Permit rules vary by project, so ask the Crowley building department whether yours needs one. The Community Development office is at 201 East Main Street, and the permits line is 817-297-2201 extension 3000, with inspection requests at extension 3020. Hours are Monday through Thursday from 7:30 am to 5:30 pm and Friday from 7:30 am to 11:30 am. Applications can also be filed through the city's MyGov portal.

What We Install

Most jobs fall into one of these categories.

Fixtures and fittings

Sinks, vanities, tubs, showers, toilets, hose bibs and washer boxes. We match the supply stops, drain traps and shutoffs to the fixture. For repairs on existing ones, see faucet repair and toilet repair.

Water heaters and appliances

Gas and electric tanks, tankless units, dishwashers, ice makers and disposals. We handle the venting, expansion tank, pan and drain. Larger jobs go through water heater installation.

Gas lines

Stub-outs for ranges, dryers, grills, fire pits and generators. Every line is pressure tested and checked for leaks before it is put into service.

Backflow and irrigation

Vacuum breakers and reduced pressure principle devices protect your drinking water from irrigation and chemical lines.

Sump and ejector pumps

Pumps with check valves and sealed lids for low spots, below-grade baths and laundry rooms.

New construction and additions

Underground rough-in, top-out and final trim for new homes, garages, guest suites and bathrooms.

Low Water Pressure: An Install Problem as Often as a Repair

Weak pressure at one fixture is usually a clogged aerator or cartridge. Weak pressure everywhere points to something upstream: a partly closed main valve, an undersized line, a failing pressure regulator or corroded galvanized pipe. Crowley water runs at 6 to 9 gpg hardness, and scale narrows old galvanized lines year after year.

A plumber measures static pressure with a gauge threaded onto a hose bib. Typical household pressure sits in the range of 40 to 80 psi. Based on the number, we may replace the regulator, resize a branch or recommend repiping if the pipe itself is the limit. Whole-house filters can also drop flow, so we check them first.

Remodel or New Construction: How the Plumbing Differs

The work looks similar on paper but runs very differently on site.

Remodel and additions

  • We work around finished walls, existing slab and live water lines.
  • Existing drains may need cutting into the slab for a new bathroom.
  • Tie-ins to older pipe may call for transition fittings.
  • The water is shut off for part of the day while we connect.

New construction

  • Underground rough-in happens before the slab is poured, so layout mistakes are costly later.
  • Top-out comes after framing, with vent and supply runs through open studs.
  • Final trim sets fixtures after drywall and paint.
  • Local IPC Section 306.2.4 calls for underground plastic drain to sit on 4 inches of granular bedding with 85 percent standard proctor compaction.

Gas Line Install and Inspection: Safety First

Gas work follows safety-first rules. A gas line is sized for the total BTU load of every appliance on it, run with approved pipe and fittings and pressure tested before anyone lights a burner. If you ever smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 first, then call a plumber.

Before a new line goes live, we check each joint with leak solution and confirm that shutoffs are accessible. For homes where a line has already been added by someone else, a gas line inspection can confirm sizing, supports and connections. We do not promise an inspection date, since that is set by the city.

How an Install Project Runs

Planned work follows a predictable order.

  1. Walk the site and discuss the scope.

    We look at where fixtures will go, how the existing lines run and what shutoffs and access points are available.

  2. Agree on the price.

    You hear a clear price before work starts, along with notes on permits and anything that could change the scope.

  3. Rough in and test.

    Pipes go in, we pressure test the supply and water test the drains, and the inspector signs off where required.

  4. Set fixtures and finish.

    We connect fixtures, check for leaks, clean up and show you the shutoffs. Before we leave, we make sure everything flows and drains.

Sump and Ejector Pumps, Backflow and Other Add-Ons

Texas homes with low spots, below-grade baths or detached garages sometimes need a pump. A sump pump moves groundwater out of a pit. An ejector pump lifts wastewater from a below-grade bath up to the sewer line. Both need a check valve, a sealed lid and a dedicated circuit.

Irrigation systems need a backflow device. Local IPC Section 608.17.5 says no valve may be placed downstream of an atmospheric vacuum breaker on lawn irrigation, and chemical injection needs a reduced pressure principal backflow preventer. We install them to those rules. Restaurants and shops with grease traps are covered under commercial plumbing.
