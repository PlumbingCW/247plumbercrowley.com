Broken Water Line: What to Look For First

Not every symptom means a broken line, but these combinations point to the buried pipe.

Wet ground, no rain

A soft spot or pooled water in the yard between the street and the house, especially on dry days.

Meter keeps spinning

With every fixture off, a moving meter dial or flowing leak indicator is water going somewhere you cannot see.

Pressure falls

A drop in pressure at every faucet at once suggests a leak or restriction before the house plumbing.

No water at all

If the neighbors have water and you do not, check the shutoff valve first, then call us.

Murky or air-spitting taps

Sputtering faucets can mean the line is drawing air through a break.

Unexplained high bill

A bill that jumps with no change in habits is worth a meter test.

Finding the Leak Without Digging Up the Yard

We start with a meter check: all fixtures off, then watch the low-flow indicator. Next we shut the main at the house and watch again. If the meter still moves, the leak is between the meter and the house, and a pressure test on the line confirms it.

To pin down the spot we use acoustic listening gear on the pipe, plus a tracer if the line is hard to follow. A pinpointed leak means a small hole, not a trench the length of the yard. Our leak detection page explains the tools in more detail, and the same methods apply to a slab leak if the break runs under the house.

Water line work follows the 2021 International Plumbing Code that Crowley has adopted. According to Public Works, ask the city where its side ends, because the standard for who maintains the line is not spelled out on the city pages.

Code and Soil: What Goes in the Ground

Crowley adopted the 2021 International Plumbing Code with local amendments. Section 305.1 in those amendments says metallic piping, except cast iron, ductile iron and galvanized steel, must not sit in direct contact with corrosive soil and must be sheathed at least 8 mil (0.008 inch) thick. In practice this means a copper line in bare clay needs a protective sleeve, and plastic pipe such as PEX or HDPE is often chosen instead.

Clay soil also moves. In this part of North Texas the ground swells when wet and shrinks when dry, which stresses rigid joints and old galvanized steel. Homes built before the mid-1990s may still have galvanized or polybutylene supply lines. Replacing a worn service line with modern flexible pipe reduces the chance of a repeat break.

Low Pressure or No Water: A Quick Triage

Before you assume the line is broken, work through these checks.

  • Is the main valve at the house fully open?
  • Is the meter shutoff open, or did a contractor close it?
  • Is the pressure drop on hot only? That points to the water heater, not the service line.
  • Are neighbors affected? If so, call Public Works. A main break or a city repair may be the cause.
  • Does a pressure reducing valve sit near the main? A failed one can choke the whole house.

If none of these explain it, the line is the next suspect. A plumber can gauge static pressure at an outdoor hose bib. House pressure in the range of 40 to 80 psi is typical, and a reading far below that with the main open points to a leak or a clogged line.
