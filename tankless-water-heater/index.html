Tankless Water Heater in Crowley, TX

A tankless water heater in Crowley, TX heats water only when you open a tap, which saves floor space and ends the 40 or 50 gallon tank that sits waiting. The catch is the water. Crowley's supply is hard enough to coat a heat exchanger with scale, and a coated exchanger loses heat, throws error codes and wears out early.

Hard Water and Tankless Heat Exchangers

Crowley's 2025 Water Quality Report lists total hardness at 108 to 150 mg/L as calcium carbonate. The report states that as 6 to 9 grains per gallon. The city buys 100 percent of its treated surface water from Fort Worth, and the pH runs 7.8 to 8.5. That is moderately hard and slightly alkaline.

Heat is where hardness bites. Calcium and magnesium come out of solution when water is heated, and a tankless heat exchanger heats water from cold to 120 degrees in seconds across narrow copper or stainless passages. Scale forms inside those passages, narrows the flow and forces the burner to work harder to reach the set temperature.

That is why a flush on a regular schedule is the single best thing you can do for a tankless unit here. Many makers call for a flush about once a year in hard water areas, and sooner if you see a drop in flow or hot water temperature. If you want to cut scale at its source, a water softener or filter on the house supply protects the heater and every other fixture.

Gas tankless units fall under the venting and combustion air standards in the 2021 International Fuel Gas Code and Mechanical Code, both of which Crowley has adopted. Ask the Crowley building department how it applies to your home.

What a Tankless Flush and Descale Involves

A proper flush takes a plumber about an hour for a typical unit, and it needs the right fittings. Skipping the service valves on a new install is the most common reason owners cannot do it.

  1. Shut off power or gas, and the water.

    We turn off the electric breaker or the gas valve, close the cold and hot isolation valves, and let the unit cool. Never flush a heater that is still hot.

  2. Connect a pump and a hose loop.

    Hoses attach to the service ports on the isolation valves, and a small pump circulates a descaling solution, most often white vinegar or a mild acid cleaner, through the heat exchanger.

  3. Circulate for 45 minutes or more.

    The solution sits and circulates while it dissolves the scale. Heavily scaled units may need longer or a repeat cycle.

  4. Rinse and clean the inlet screen.

    We flush the unit with clean water to remove all solution and loosened scale, then clean the inlet water filter and check for leaks.

  5. Test flow and temperature.

    We reopen the valves, restore power or gas, run a hot tap and check the temperature rise and error history. Then we log the date so you know when the next flush is due.

Tankless or Tank: Which Fits Your House

Tankless is not right for every home. Here is an honest look at both.

Tankless makes sense when

  • You want to free floor space and avoid standby heat loss.
  • You can run a larger gas line or heavy electric circuit.
  • You have a regular flush plan and accept the annual cost.
  • You plan to stay in the home a long time. Tankless units often last around 20 years.

A tank may be better when

  • You want the lowest upfront cost and simplest install.
  • Your venting or gas line would need major changes.
  • You never maintain equipment and hard water is not addressed.
  • A tank, with an 8 to 12 year life, fits your budget this year.
