Is a Softener Worth It for Your House?

Because the water meets standards, this is a cost and comfort question. Here is a fair way to weigh it.

A softener may pay off if

  • You see scale on faucets and shower doors within a week of cleaning.
  • You run a tank water heater and want to slow sediment buildup.
  • Dry skin, stiff laundry or heavy soap use bother your household.
  • You plan to stay in the house long enough to recover the cost.

You may not need one if

  • Your main complaint is taste or smell, which a carbon filter addresses better.
  • You prefer to skip the salt and the tank maintenance.
  • You are short on space or plan to move soon.
  • A single showerhead or faucet filter would solve what bothers you.

Softener, Conditioner or Filter: Picking the Right Tool

These products are often lumped together, but they solve different problems.

Salt-based softener

Uses ion-exchange resin to swap calcium and magnesium for sodium or potassium. It is the only option that truly removes hardness, and it needs salt and a drain connection.

Salt-free conditioner

Changes how scale forms rather than removing it. Results vary, and it does not give the slick feel of softened water.

Whole-house carbon filter

Reduces chlorine and chloramine taste and odor. Catalytic carbon is usually needed for chloramine.

Reverse osmosis at the sink

A small under-sink system for drinking and cooking water. It treats a single tap rather than the whole house.

How a Softener Gets Installed

A typical job fits in half a day when the plumbing is accessible.

  1. Choose the spot.

    The unit needs a level pad, a 120 volt outlet, a drain within reach and room for a bag of salt. A garage near the main line is common.

  2. Tie into the main.

    We shut off the water, cut into the main line after the hose bibs you want left untreated, and install a bypass valve so you can isolate the unit.

  3. Run the drain.

    The regeneration drain needs an air gap so wastewater cannot flow back into the unit. Brine and rinse water go to a floor drain or standpipe.

  4. Program and test.

    We set hardness, capacity and regeneration time, then check for leaks and test the treated water.

Backflow Testing and Irrigation Fixtures

Backflow preventers keep dirty water from being drawn back into the clean supply. Crowley's local amendment, Section 608.17.5, says no valve may be installed downstream of an atmospheric vacuum breaker on a lawn irrigation system, and chemical injection calls for a reduced pressure principal backflow preventer. If you have a sprinkler system or a chemical feed, the device and its placement matter.

Testing requirements for a given device vary, so ask the city whether and how often yours must be tested. We can test and repair common assemblies, and we can replace one that fails. For other plumbing work around your home, our plumbing installation page lists what we install.

Repairing an Existing Softener or Conditioner

Many calls are not new installs. The usual faults are a stuck valve head, a clogged injector, a salt bridge in the brine tank or a resin bed that has worn out. A softener that stops regenerating gives you hard water without any warning light.

Other problems we see are overflow from a clogged drain line or a brine tank that has flooded, and a bypass valve that was left open. If you drop something into the brine well, do not reach into it. Call us and we will retrieve it safely.

If a leaking unit is flooding the garage, shut the bypass or the main valve and call. A flooded floor from a softener is a leak problem first and a treatment problem second.
