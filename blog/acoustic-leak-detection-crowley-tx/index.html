Acoustic Leak Detection: How Hidden Leaks Get Found in Crowley

Acoustic leak detection finds a hidden leak by listening to the pipe. A plumber presses a ground microphone or contact sensor on the floor, wall or meter, and a pressurized line with a break makes a hiss that a healthy line does not. For a Crowley, TX home, that usually means the leak can be narrowed to a few feet without cutting any concrete.

It works best on supply lines under pressure, so it is the right tool when your meter keeps spinning with every fixture off. Tracer gas, thermal imaging and pressure testing fill in the cases that listening alone cannot settle. Below is how each one works, what you can safely check yourself, and when to stop and call.

How Listening Finds a Leak Through Concrete

Water escaping a pinhole under pressure makes noise. The sound travels along the pipe wall, through the soil and up through the slab, and it is loudest directly above the break. A ground microphone picks it up, and a handheld unit with headphones and a screen shows pitch and volume.

Before anyone listens, the house has to be quiet. Every fixture is off, the ice maker is paused, the irrigation controller is stopped and the washer is idle. The main valve is opened fully so the line holds its normal household pressure, often somewhere between 40 and 80 psi.

The technician then walks a grid across the floor and marks the loudest spots in chalk. Sound can ride a copper line for twenty feet or more, so one reading proves nothing. Two or three marks that line up along a pipe run are what point to the leak.

A listening test is a diagnostic method, not a code standard, so the repair that follows still has to meet the plumbing code. Crowley has adopted the 2021 International Plumbing Code.

Four Methods, Four Different Jobs

No single tool finds every leak. A good leak detection visit often combines two of these, because each one is strong where another is weak.

Acoustic listening

Best for pressurized supply lines under a slab or behind a finished wall. It cannot hear a drain line, because a drain is not under pressure, and it struggles on plastic pipe that muffles sound.

Tracer gas

A harmless mix of hydrogen and nitrogen, typically about 5 percent hydrogen, is pushed into an isolated line. The gas escapes through the break and a sniffer finds it at the surface. It suits plastic pipe and slow leaks that barely hiss.

Thermal imaging

An infrared camera shows temperature differences on a surface. A leaking hot line leaves a warm patch on the floor, and a wet wall reads cooler than dry drywall. It needs a gap of a few degrees to read clearly.

Pressure testing

Test plugs isolate one section of pipe and a gauge watches it. If the needle falls with nothing running, the leak is inside that section. This confirms a leak exists and tells us where to point the microphone.

What You Can Safely Check Before We Arrive

A few minutes of checking makes the visit faster and tells us what to bring. None of this needs tools.

  • Read the meter.With every fixture off, watch the small triangle or dial for ten minutes. Any movement means water is leaving the system somewhere.
  • Feel the floor.Walk barefoot across tile or slab. A patch that stays warm when no hot water is running often sits over a leaking hot line.
  • Listen at night.Stand near the water heater and the main shutoff in a quiet house. A faint hiss or tick that never stops is useful information.
  • Note the pattern.Write down whether the bill rose slowly or jumped, and whether hot or cold taps sound different. Hot-only and cold-only clues change the plan.
  • Find the main valve.Make sure you can turn it off by hand. If it will not turn or drips at the stem, tell us when you call.

When to Stop Looking and Call

Stop if you see water near an outlet, a panel or any electrical device. Keep people and pets away from that area and shut the main water valve if you can reach it without stepping through water. Do not open walls or break tile to chase a sound.

Also call if the floor feels warm in a wide patch, if a wall is bowing or if you smell mustiness that returns after cleaning. Those point to a leak that has been running long enough to soak materials. If the problem is active, call (866) 310-6010 and we can dispatch day or night.

What a Plumber Does Once the Leak Is Located

Finding the leak is only half the job. With a chalk mark on the slab and a pressure reading in hand, the plumber explains the options before any work starts: a spot repair, a reroute through the attic or walls, or a section of new pipe. You get a clear price before work starts.

If the break is under the foundation, the next step is usually slab leak repair, which can mean a small tunnel or a bypass line. If it is in the yard between the meter and the house, a water line repair with a short trench is more typical. Your plumber can walk you through which applies.

We work across Crowley and also take calls from neighbors in Burleson, Everman and Forest Hill. Wherever the house is, the sequence stays the same: confirm, listen, verify with a second method, then mark and repair.
